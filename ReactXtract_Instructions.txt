As a professional chemist, analyze the provided chemical reaction literature and extract information only from the supplied article and supporting information.
 
For each distinct reaction, identify:
1. Products
2. Starting materials
3. Reagents
4. Catalysts
5. Solvents
6. Reaction time
7. Reaction pressure
8. Other reaction conditions
9. Number of reaction stages
 
## Original Rules

- Do not infer information that is not explicitly supported by the source.
- Distinguish starting materials from reagents based on their role in the
  transformation and their incorporation into the product.
- Identify catalysts separately from reagents.
- Identify all solvents used in the reaction.
- Report reaction time exactly as stated and preserve the original units.
- Report reaction pressure only when stated or unambiguously specified.
- Include temperature, atmosphere, irradiation, microwave conditions,
  electrochemical conditions, concentration, equivalents, catalyst loading,
  and other relevant experimental conditions under "Other reaction conditions."
- Count sequential chemical transformations as separate reaction stages.
- Do not count work-up, extraction, filtration, evaporation, or purification
  as reaction stages.
- If information is unavailable, leave the field empty. Return an empty array
  for unavailable list fields and an empty string for unavailable scalar fields.
- Do not infer missing values from similar reactions, general chemical knowledge,
  or other publications.
- If the article contains multiple distinct reactions, create a separate
  reaction record for each.
- Preserve the source wording for chemical names where possible.
- Preserve chemical typography in extracted field values. Use Unicode subscript
  digits for molecular-formula indices (for example H₂O, Na₂SO₄, CH₂Cl₂,
  and CuSO₄·5H₂O) and Unicode superscripts for explicitly shown charges or
  isotopes. Do not turn locants, compound identifiers, quantities, times, page
  numbers, or temperatures into subscripts (for example keep 1,2-dichloro,
  compound 3a, 20 mL, 24 h, and 80 °C unchanged).

## Additional Rules

- Search every supplied source type for reactions: graphical abstracts,
  numbered and unnumbered schemes, reaction-condition tables, optimization
  tables, substrate-scope tables, narrative results, experimental sections,
  general procedures, representative procedures, and supporting information.
- Extract preparative reactions that produce intermediates as well as reactions
  that produce final target compounds. Do not limit extraction to the paper's
  headline or biologically tested products.
- Treat each product-forming scheme arrow or explicitly described transformation
  as a reaction. When an arrow bundles sequential transformations without an
  isolated intermediate, keep it as one record and report the stated number of
  stages. When distinct intermediates or products are shown, create separate
  records for the distinct transformations.
- When a source explicitly states that several labeled compounds were prepared
  by a general or representative procedure, create a separate record for every
  labeled compound. Apply the shared procedure only where the source explicitly
  links that example or compound series to it, and preserve product-specific
  yields or conditions.
- In reaction-scope or optimization tables, create separate records for distinct
  successful product-forming entries when each entry has its own substrates,
  conditions, or outcome. Do not create reaction records for entries explicitly
  reported as failed, trace, or no reaction.
- Scheme-only reactions are valid evidence. Use source labels such as
  "compound 12" when a chemical name is unavailable; never invent a name from
  a drawn structure.
- Do not misclassify retrosynthetic arrows, proposed mechanisms, biological
  assays, docking experiments, formulation steps, analytical measurements, or
  work-up and purification operations as preparative reactions.
- Before returning, re-scan all supplied pages and compare every scheme arrow,
  procedure heading, intermediate label, and product label against the output.
  Add any supported reaction that is missing, without duplicating the same
  transformation described in multiple places.

### Per-Reaction Role Check

Before returning each reaction record, validate the role of every listed
chemical against that specific transformation and its cited source evidence:

- Product: include only a substance explicitly formed, obtained, afforded,
  isolated, or shown at the product end of the applicable reaction arrow. Do
  not treat a compound as a product merely because it is discussed, tested, or
  characterized elsewhere.
- Starting material: include only a reactant present before the transformation
  that is explicitly consumed or incorporated into the reported product. Do
  not infer incorporation solely from general chemical knowledge.
- Reagent: include a substance used to cause or enable the transformation that
  is not functioning as the catalyst, reaction solvent, starting material, or
  product. Exclude quenching agents, extraction liquids, washes, drying agents,
  chromatography eluents, and recrystallization solvents unless the source
  explicitly uses them during the reaction itself.
- Catalyst: include a substance only when the source identifies it as a
  catalyst or explicitly reports catalytic loading or use. Do not infer a
  catalytic role from chemical plausibility alone.
- Solvent: include only the reaction medium. Exclude solvents used solely for
  work-up, extraction, washing, drying, chromatography, or recrystallization.
- Do not duplicate a chemical across role lists unless the source explicitly
  supports a genuine dual role in that reaction.
- For every populated role field, confirm that its evidence quote supports both
  the chemical identity and its assigned role in this reaction. If the identity
  is reported but the role is ambiguous, do not guess the category. If no
  supported chemicals remain for a role, return an empty array for that field
  and set its confidence to 0.
- Field confidence must reflect confidence in both the extracted value and the
  assigned role. The mere presence of a chemical name on the page is not enough
  for a high confidence score.
